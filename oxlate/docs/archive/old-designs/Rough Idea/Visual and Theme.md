## The direction I would lock for Oxlate

I would **not** make Oxlate a pure black-and-white website.

I would make it a **near-monochrome architectural system**:

> **Warm Ivory + Deep Black + Graphite + very restrained Bronze**

That gives you the classy look you want without making the site sterile.

The important distinction is:

**Black & white should be the identity.
Bronze should be the material.
Ivory should be the environment.**

Not:

**black + white + bronze + silver + gray + multiple metallic effects.**

That becomes too many themes competing.

---

# 1. Black and white absolutely can work

Actually, black and white is one of the strongest choices for your situation.

It gives you:

* seriousness
* confidence
* editorial character
* technical precision
* timelessness
* strong typography
* strong contrast
* less "cheap agency" feeling

And because Oxlate is new, that's useful.

A colorful site can accidentally make you look like:

> another web development company.

Black/white allows you to build a **recognizable visual language**.

But there is a flaw with going *literally* black and white everywhere.

### Pure black + pure white becomes harsh.

You lose the subtle hierarchy that makes luxury interfaces feel expensive.

Compare:

```text
#000000
#FFFFFF
```

with:

```text
#171717
#F5F2EC
#686861
#DDD8CF
```

The second feels substantially more sophisticated because there are tonal steps.

So I wouldn't abandon black and white.

I would make it:

> **Black and ivory, supported by graphite and warm neutrals.**

That's the better version.

# 5. Your bronze is good, but don't let it become "gold luxury"

Your `#A87445` direction is actually very good.

But bronze has a narrow operating range.

Too much:

> luxury hotel / jewelry / investment company

Too little:

> why did we even add it?

I'd use it at approximately:

**2–5% of visible UI area.**

For example:

* CTA
* active navigation
* section number
* tiny architectural marker
* selected project state
* hover line

Not:

* bronze cards
* bronze backgrounds
* bronze headings everywhere
* bronze borders around everything

Bronze should feel like a **detail discovered**, not the dominant theme.

# 7. The logo using Orbitron is fine

Actually, I would keep Orbitron **strictly for the logo/brand mark**.

That's where it has value.

Orbitron communicates:

> technology

But if you use it throughout the interface, it starts communicating:

> gaming / crypto / futuristic startup / cyberpunk.

That's the opposite of what you're trying to achieve.

So:

### Oxlate logo

Orbitron / custom lettering

### Headings

Satoshi or Geist

### Body

Inter or Geist

### Numbers / technical metadata

Possibly Orbitron

That's a strong hierarchy.

I personally lean toward **Geist throughout the interface** for the cleaner, more contemporary direction.

Satoshi will feel slightly more designed/editorial.

So you essentially have:

**Geist = precision**

**Satoshi = premium/editorial**

Both are viable.

# 8. Your rounded corners need to be reconsidered

Your documentation says:

> Rounded corners (12–18px)

I wouldn't apply that globally.

The screenshot you're showing has an architectural identity.

Very rounded cards will fight that language.

I'd do:

### Major containers

0–8px

### Buttons

2–6px

### Image corners

0–8px

### Small floating UI

8–12px only when necessary

The more rectangular geometry you use, the stronger the architectural language becomes.

You can still have softness through **ivory, shadows and spacing** rather than rounded corners.

# 10. And this changes how you should use images

Yes.

**Custom generated images can absolutely work.**

But I would be very careful with them.

Your instinct is right that generic agency photography would weaken the site.

I would not use:

> businessman working on laptop
> developers in office
> diverse team smiling
> close-up keyboard
> woman holding phone

Those immediately tell the visitor:

> generic agency.

Instead, create a custom visual language.

For example:

### Architectural photography

Concrete, glass, stone, metal, shadows, staircases, structural geometry.

### Abstract technical objects

Servers, interfaces, circuitry, materials, dimensional forms—but extremely minimal.

### Custom 3D compositions

A bronze/black object interacting with an ivory environment.

### Interface + architecture

A product interface projected onto or integrated with architectural surfaces.

### Macro materials

Brushed metal, paper, glass, stone, blackened steel.

The visual message becomes:

> **We care about how things are constructed.**

Which supports your positioning beautifully.

---

# 11. AI-generated imagery can actually become an advantage

But only if you **art-direct it heavily**.

Don't use:

> "premium technology company office, cinematic, 8k"

That will produce generic AI agency wallpaper.

Instead establish a **visual art direction**.

For example:

> Warm ivory architectural environment, deep black structural geometry, restrained oxidized bronze detail, directional sunlight, hard geometric shadows, no people, no text, editorial architectural photography, large negative space, tactile materials, understated industrial precision.

Then every generated image belongs to the same universe.

That's much stronger.

And I'd use **the same treatment across every image**.

So your website eventually has an unmistakable visual signature.

---

# 12. I would avoid AI-generated people almost entirely

Especially at this stage.

People generated by AI introduce questions about authenticity.

A visitor may consciously or subconsciously think:

> Is this fake?

You don't want that question anywhere near a brand whose biggest problem is already lack of track record.

Architecture, objects, materials and product interfaces don't have that problem.

You want the website to say:

> **Everything here was deliberately constructed.**

# 14. Don't make every element interactive

You specifically said:

> “the user can't interact by the animation and everything”

I think that's correct.

Don't turn this into a website where users have to:

* drag objects
* rotate 3D models
* click mysterious elements
* hover everything
* explore hidden content

That gets old extremely quickly.

The user should be able to:

**scroll → read → understand → inspect work → contact.**

The animation accompanies that journey.

It doesn't become a game.

---

# 15. One design rule I'd establish now

This should become a strict Oxlate rule:

> **Every decorative element must have a structural reason to exist.**

That's a very useful constraint.

For example:

A line?

It aligns something.

A number?

It indicates sequence.

A box?

It frames something.

A diagonal?

It represents structure or transition.

Bronze?

It indicates importance.

Animation?

It represents construction or movement.

If an element doesn't have a reason, remove it.

That will keep your site premium.

# And I would define the theme in one sentence

Your current documentation says a lot of good things, but the design team needs one sentence they can use to judge every decision.

I'd make it:

> **Oxlate is an architectural editorial interface for technology — warm, monochrome, precise, and quietly premium.**

Then every design decision gets tested against that.

Does a gradient fit?

Probably not.

Does a neon blue button fit?

No.

Does an enormous glossy 3D AI robot fit?

No.

Does a black structural line crossing an ivory field fit?

Absolutely.

Does a carefully art-directed architectural image fit?

Yes.

Does a bronze micro-accent fit?

Yes.

That's how you maintain consistency.

And one final point: **I would not make the website look "expensive" by adding more effects.**

For Oxlate, expensive should come from:

**proportion + typography + whitespace + materiality + composition + motion discipline.**

That is much harder to fake, and it perfectly matches the credibility problem we discussed earlier.

Your current screenshot is actually heading toward a **very distinctive identity**. I wouldn't throw it away. I'd do the opposite: **strip 20–30% of the visual noise, strengthen the architectural system, and make the black/ivory/bronze relationship brutally consistent.**

That could become the thing people recognize as **Oxlate**, rather than just another premium agency website.
